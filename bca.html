What Is It?

The Great Wall of China is the longest man-made structure ever constructed — a vast network of walls, watchtowers, beacon towers, garrison stations, and fortifications stretching across the mountains, deserts, and plains of northern China. Rather than a single continuous barrier, it is a collection of walls built and rebuilt across many centuries by successive dynasties.

At its most imposing, the wall stands up to 9 metres (30 feet) tall and nearly 5 metres (15 feet) wide at the base — broad enough for five horses to ride side by side. Watchtowers rise every few hundred metres, and soldiers could signal danger across vast distances using smoke by day and fire by night.

"You are not a true hero until you have climbed the Great Wall." — Chinese Proverb

Location & History

The wall stretches roughly east to west across northern China, from Shanhaiguan on the Bohai Sea in the east all the way to Jiayuguan in the Gobi Desert in the west, passing through provinces including Hebei, Beijing, Shanxi, Shaanxi, and Gansu. Its path closely follows China's historic northern frontier.

Construction began as early as the 7th century BC, when regional Chinese states built walls to defend against each other and against nomadic northern peoples. After unifying China in 221 BC, Emperor Qin Shi Huang famously ordered these scattered walls linked into a single great barrier. Later dynasties — the Han, Sui, Jin, and especially the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644) — massively expanded and reinforced the structure. The iconic brick-faced sections tourists visit today were built primarily during the Ming era.

Historical Importance

The Great Wall served as far more than a military fortification. It functioned as a customs boundary regulating trade along the Silk Road, a communication corridor spanning enormous distances, and a powerful symbol of Chinese civilisation's unity and power. For centuries it defined the boundary between the agricultural Han Chinese heartland and the nomadic steppe cultures to the north.

Today the Great Wall stands as one of the greatest engineering feats in human history and an enduring cultural icon. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 and voted one of the New Seven Wonders of the World in 2007. It remains the most visited monument in China and one of the most recognised landmarks on Earth.

Fascinating Facts

  • The total length of all Great Wall sections across all dynasties exceeds 21,196 kilometres — long enough to circle the Earth more than halfway.
  • An estimated 400,000 or more workers died during construction. Many were soldiers, peasants, and convicted criminals conscripted into labour.
  • Contrary to a popular legend, the Great Wall cannot be seen from space with the naked eye — NASA astronauts have confirmed this myth is false.
  • Ming Dynasty builders used sticky rice paste mixed into the mortar as a binding agent, making those sections remarkably durable even 600 years later.
  • The wall has over 30,000 watchtowers along its full length, enabling soldiers to relay warning signals across hundreds of kilometres within hours.
  • The most visited section — Badaling near Beijing — has welcomed more than 300 million tourists since opening, including over 400 foreign heads of state and government.
  • Significant sections of the wall have been lost to erosion, farming, and looting for building materials — it is estimated that only 30% of the Ming-era wall remains in good condition.
